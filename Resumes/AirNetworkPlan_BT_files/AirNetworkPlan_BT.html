Step 5 — Design the Operational Process
Handover: Where is cargo handed over? Example: PhoenixExp truck arrives at the Cathay Cargo Terminal in Hong Kong. Who receives the cargo, who signs acceptance, and when does responsibility transfer? Cut-off: Cargo acceptance at 18:00 and flight departure at 21:00. Can PhoenixExp consistently meet the cut-off? Missed flight: Define recovery before launch: rebook the next flight, reroute through ICN, or use charter backup. A good implementation anticipates failures.
Step 6 — Systems Integration
Shipment visibility: Confirm that received, loaded, departed, arrived, and delivered updates appear correctly. If tracking disappears, Customer Service becomes blind. Data exchange: Booking information, flight schedules, status messages, and exception notifications may move through EDI, cargo messaging platforms, or airline interfaces. Financial capture: Confirm that invoices can be reconciled so cost control does not fail.
Step 7 — Build Standard Operating Procedures
Cargo Acceptance SOP: Documents required, verification owner, and action for incomplete documentation. Disruption SOP: Notification, escalation, recovery-plan approval, and ownership when a flight is delayed. Dangerous Goods SOP: Storage, inspection, and regulatory requirements. The goal is no improvisation during live operations.

  • Contingency plan established
Only after all boxes are checked should launch proceed.
Step 9 — Test the End-to-End Process
Booking test Can shipments be booked correctly? Tracking test Do status messages appear? Recovery test Can the process handle disruptions? Billing test Are charges calculated correctly? Many hidden problems are found here, which is why testing is critical.

Once performance stabilizes, ownership transfers to operational teams. The partner is now part of the permanent network.

Probability, Distributions & Optimization
📐
Probability, Distributions & Optimization
Air Network Partner Management
📊 Convert uncertainty into measurable network, capacity, risk, and partner decisions.
Why this matters: Air Network Planning is not only about finding airline partners and arranging capacity. Future volume, delays, capacity availability, weather, and customer demand are uncertain. Probability, statistical distributions, and optimization provide a disciplined basis for network decisions.
1. Probability Theory in Air Network Planning
Core question Instead of asking, “What do I think will happen?”, a network engineer asks, “What is the probability that this will happen?” Examples
  • Probability demand exceeds available capacity.
  • Probability of service failure or missed delivery commitments.
  • Probability of network disruption during typhoon season.
  • Probability of partner capacity shortages during peak season.
Vietnam–USA lane example Average demand = 50 tons/day · Available capacity = 55 tons/day The decision is to quantify the probability that daily demand exceeds capacity and determine whether additional capacity should be purchased.
2. Statistical Distribution Functions
Why distributions matter: Transit times, demand volumes, delays, and capacity utilization vary. An average alone can hide the operational risk. Example: SGN → HKG → MEM averages 36 hours, but the distribution may be: 20% arrive in 34 hours · 50% in 36 hours · 20% in 38 hours · 10% after 42 hours. The distribution shows the range and likelihood of outcomes.
DistributionNetwork applicationsDecision support
NormalDaily volume, transit time, capacity utilizationEstimate capacity exceedance; build safety buffers; forecast peaks
PoissonShipment arrivals, operational incidents, service failuresPredict arrival patterns, disruption events, workforce needs
ExponentialTime between disruptions, equipment failures, service incidentsReliability analysis and network resilience planning
BinomialSuccess/failure outcomes and on-time performanceEstimate the probability that 18 or more of 20 flights arrive on time
3. Forecasting Demand Using Probability
Network planning is fundamentally a forecasting function. The operating question is not only “What is the average demand?” but also “What demand scenarios and risk levels should the network support?”
ScenarioDemandProbability
Low45 T/day20%
Base55 T/day60%
High70 T/day20%
🎯 Management must decide whether to plan for average demand, protect against the worst case, or accept a defined level of risk. This is a probability-based capacity decision.

5. Optimization in Air Network Management
Optimization finds the best solution while satisfying operational constraints. Maximize
  • Service reliability
  • Network resilience
  • Capacity utilization
Minimize
  • Cost
  • Transit time
  • Service failures

7. Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA)
Partner selection rarely depends on cost alone. A weighted model makes tradeoffs transparent and creates data-driven recommendations.
CriteriaWeight
Service Performance30%
Capacity Availability25%
Transit Time25%
Cost20%
Score = Σ(Weight × Rating). The result is a transparent recommendation that balances cost, service, capacity, and transit time.
8. Capacity Optimization
Question: How should 50 tons/day of available cargo volume be distributed among Cathay, EVA, and Korean Air? Objective: Allocate volume to Minimize cost while maintaining service quality. Constraints: Capacity limits · Transit requirements · Reliability targets · Peak-season restrictions This is a classic Operations Research problem.
